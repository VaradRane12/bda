Part 3 · The trick

Weigh the pieces to find what broke

If a vase breaks, weighing all the pieces tells you how heavy it was. Physicists do the same: from the two muons they calculate the mass of the particle they came from. One collision tells you nothing, but thousands reveal a pattern. Try it with real collisions from our dataset:

The results: six charts you can play with

Every chart is drawn from our Spark results on the real data. Hover (or tap) to see exact numbers.

Chart 1 · Histogram

Every spike is a particle

  • Left to right: how heavy the parent particle was. Up: how many collisions had that weight.
  • The smooth slope is random muon pairs (background).
  • Click a label on a spike to meet that particle.

Chart 2 · Bar chart

How sure are we each one is real?

  • σ (sigma) measures how sure we are a spike isn't just luck.
  • Physicists only say "discovery" past the 5σ line: a fluke chance of about 1 in 3.5 million.
In one line: 7 of the 8 particles are confirmed; Υ(3S) is a strong hint that needs more data.
Chart 3 · Advanced: time-series

Does the data stay consistent?

  • The detector recorded our data in 15 sessions during 2011, shown in time order.
  • We count each particle per 1,000 collisions, so big and small sessions compare fairly.
  • Click the coloured buttons to show or hide a particle.
In one line: what gets saved changes a lot over the year, most likely because the trigger was re-tuned.
Chart 4 · Advanced: heatmap

Where do muons hit the detector?

  • Picture the detector as a tin can, cut open and laid flat.
  • Left to right: from one lid (endcap), through the middle tube (barrel), to the other lid. Bottom to top: around it.
  • Darker = more muons. Hover a square for its count.
In one line: even all the way around, so no blind spots; about half the muons hit the middle.
Chart 5 · Line chart

Which part measures best?

  • The same particle (J/ψ) measured with muons from different parts of the detector.
  • A perfect detector would draw a thin line. Real ones blur it: narrower = more precise.
In one line: the middle of the detector measures 1.8× more sharply than its ends.
Chart 6 · Advanced: network graph

Which particles are seen where?

  • Particles on the left, the three parts of the detector on the right.
  • Thicker line = more collisions linking them. Hover a circle to light up its links.
In one line: heavy particles spray their pieces wider, so studying them needs the whole detector.
Part 6 · Takeaways

What we learned

8 particles

found hiding in the data; 7 pass the 5σ "discovery" bar.

0.55 %

the biggest error in where a spike sits: like a kitchen scale off by under 6 g per kg.

35 rows

out of 100,000 were faulty and removed. The rest is clean, with no missing values.

5×

more Z bosons per 1,000 collisions by the end of 2011: what gets saved changes over time.

No blind spots

muons are spread evenly all around the detector.

1.8× sharper

measurements in the middle of the detector than at its ends.

What we recommend

📏 Compare fairly

Always use "per 1,000 collisions", never raw totals, and keep the time-series as a live alarm for sudden jumps.

🎯 Use the middle for precision

Measure masses with barrel-only collisions; use the whole detector when counting rare particles.

➕ Collect ~1.5× more data

That should push Υ(3S) past the 5σ discovery bar.

🚀 Scale up the same code

The Spark code runs unchanged on a big cluster for data thousands of times larger.
